# Check of the "new" storm nights (text + literature)

Date checked: 2026-11-12. Task A uses only the local dossier files (results/dossiers/round*/*.tsv and .txt). Task B uses web sources; every source is linked below. Quotes are copied from the OCR exactly as it reads, errors included.

## Task A: does the raw text support the southernmost claim?

| Night | Claimed southernmost | Article ID | Quote (OCR) | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1860-08-06/07 | Pike County, MS 31.2N | 1860-08-13_06 (1860-08-21_01 is the same text again) | "display of northern lights the morning of the Tth 2 At2s A. NJ" | VERIFIED, with an OCR caveat. This is a first-hand letter in the New Orleans Daily Crescent (published 1860-08-22), headed "PIKE COUNTY, Miss, Aug, Ii". The OCR gives "Tth" (7th) and "2½ A.M.", so the sighting falls on the night of Aug 6-7. The aurora was in the north, "east to west", seen "notwithstanding the bright moonlight". The same row is in the tsv twice under two IDs, so count it once. Next one south: 1860-08-13_21 (Richmond Dispatch), "A brilliant aurora boreaiis was seen in Charleston, S. c., on Tuesday night". That is a one-line secondhand report for Tuesday Aug 7, so its obs_date of 08-07 is correct. |
| 1851-09-03/04 | Savannah, GA coast 32.0N | 1851-09-17_04 | "early the mornn Of Wednesday, the 4th instant" and "watches ... point ing about three o'clock" | VERIFIED. This is a detailed first-hand letter dated "SAVANNAH, Sept. 8, 1851" and reprinted in the Weekly National Intelligencer. The party saw it from an island plantation on the coast, at about 3 am. It reached 45 degrees high with green columns and pale red streamers. The weekday does not match: Sept 4, 1851 was a Thursday. Either way it is the night of Sept 3-4. |
| 1861-03-09 | Oxford, MS 34.4N | 1861-03-09_00 | "Northern Lights made brilliant the North ern horizon on Saturday night last" | VERIFIED, but the place comes from the paper's title. The paper is the Oxford Intelligencer, published Wed 1861-03-13, so "Saturday night last" is Mar 9. The header in the file gives the location as "?, MS; lat 33.1", and the 34.4N comes from a correction to the paper's location. The item is short and gives no details. |
| 1837-07-01 | Cheraw, SC 34.7N | 1837-07-04_06 | "was seen from Our town on Satur- day evening last, about 8 o'clock" | VERIFIED. This is a local Cheraw Gazette item, published Wed 1837-07-05, so "Saturday" is July 1. It says it was "for some hours after" and adds "no recollection ... so far South". Charlotte backs it up: 1837-07-04_02 has "observed this place the night Of the lsl instant" and a long description. The literature has a sighting further south: AJS 33:143 says it was seen "as far south as Columbus, Ga. (lat. about 32° 35')". |
| 1839-09-03 | New Orleans 30.0N | 1839-09-10_10 (also _14_09 and _15_06) | "Aurora Bofeas was beautifully brilliant al New Orleans the evening oil The Sd" | PARTLY. Every New Orleans row is secondhand. The Lynchburg Virginian (published 09-23) carries a one-line news item: fire bells were rung and the light was great. The New Orleans True American item (1839-09-10_07, also filed as _05_09) only mocks the Bee's description "which appeared on the 81 instant". "81" could be OCR for "3d" or "8th", and the item gives no details. The claim is plausible, and Herrick (AJS 1840) calls the display visible "above the latitude of 30°". But this archive has no local first-hand account from New Orleans. The next ones south are Bermuda, 32.3N ("plainly seen ... very brilliant", reprinted from the Bermudian, 1839-09-10_09) and Edgefield SC, 33.8N (a local, detailed account, 1839-09-05_04). |

## Task B: literature status

The Silverman New England catalogue (NSSDC SPIO-00375) is at https://spdf.gsfc.nasa.gov/pub/data/aaa_special-purpose-datasets/aaa_historical_aurora/new_england_auroral_obs_1720_1948/. I downloaded files ane800t, ane850t, ane860t and ane865t and searched them for these dates.

I fetched Lockwood et al. 2025, MNRAS, "The 2024 May event in the context of auroral activity over the past 375 yr", https://doi.org/10.1093/mnras/staf827 (PDF: https://nora.nerc.ac.uk/id/eprint/539774/). Its Table 2, the ranked list of great auroral events, contains none of the seven nights. Its closest entries are 1848 Nov 17 and the 1859 events. Only the first 100k characters of the PDF were read, which includes Table 2.

Other modern overviews I checked contain none of these nights:
- Boteler 2021, SWSC timeline table, https://doi.org/10.1051/swsc/2021011
- Hayakawa et al. 2018, ApJ 869:57 (1859), https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/aae47c
- Vázquez, Vaquero & Gallego, aurora 1700-1905, arXiv:1309.1502
- Blake et al. 2020 (Rome 1859), https://doi.org/10.1029/2019JA027336

| 1839-09-03 | CATALOGUE | Herrick, E.C., "Observations on the Aurora Borealis of September 3, 1839", AJS 38 (1840) pp. 260ff. It says the display "was seen in all parts of the United States ... visible ... above the latitude of 30°". The same volume, pp. 146ff, has "Auroras and Sunset" (Rochester, Brown Co. Ill., Olean). Scan: https://archive.org/details/americanjournalo38183940newh . The New England catalogue has 12 entries; Boston says "Splendid red Aurora in almost every direction ... also splendid in London". No modern study found. Given these sources, it is a good candidate for a pre-1840s great storm. |
| 1851-09-03/04 | CATALOGUE | Prescott, History, Theory and Practice of the Electric Telegraph (1860), pp. 318-326: "In September, 1851 ... a remarkable aurora, which took complete possession of all the telegraph lines" (https://www.earlyradiohistory.us/1860auro.htm). The NRCan space-weather "Chronology of effects" repeats this as an 1851 entry, citing Prescott 1866 (https://www.meteospatiale.gc.ca/tech/se-chr-en.php). The New England catalogue has 10 entries for Sept 3-4. No modern study found. |
| 1852-01-19 | CATALOGUE | The New England catalogue has 12 entries for Jan 19-20, including Nantucket "Splendid crimson aurora at 11 p.m." and Burlington "arch and streamers". Do not confuse it with the 1852 telegraph aurora: Prescott and NRCan date that one Feb 19, 1852, not Jan 19. No modern study found. |
| 1860-08-06 to 08-09 | CATALOGUE | The New England catalogue has 22 entries for Aug 6-9. Boston, Aug 6: "Brilliant Aurora in night, and morning of 7th". Boston, Aug 8: "telegraph wires affected, poles reversed". The local Leavenworth item about the telegraph working on auroral current alone (1860-08-13_03) agrees with this. No modern study found. Prescott's 1860 text covers only 1859 and earlier. |
| 1861-03-09 | CATALOGUE | The New England catalogue has 8 entries for Mar 9-10 (Middletown, Boston, Mendon, New Bedford "bright northern illumination ... about midnight", Providence "with streamers"). No modern study found. Love 2014, "Auroral omens of the American Civil War", Weatherwise 67(5):34, https://doi.org/10.1080/00431672.2014.939912, might be relevant, but only its abstract could be read and that does not mention March 1861. |
| 1865-08-02/03 | CATALOGUE | The New England catalogue has 9 entries. Boston, Aug 2: "Perfect white auroral arch ... through magnetic zenith". Boston, Aug 3: "Brilliant coloured streamer Aurora ... telegraph messages sent by the storm, without any battery". No modern study found in these searches. |

"Not found" means that standard web searches and the papers listed above did not turn up a modern paper devoted to the event. It does not mean no such paper exists. I could not search the Silverman low-latitude global catalogue (NOAA) or Loomis's later AJS papers for 1860-61. Loomis, AJS 1860-61, would be the next thing to check for August 1860 and March 1861.
